The three-body problem is a very famous problem in Newtonian gravity, whose goal is to compute the trajectories of three bodies subject to mutual gravitational attraction. While the two-body problem has a simple solution, the three body problem is famous for not having a closed-form analytical solution. This fact has inspired the science fiction novel by Liu Cixin, and TV series derived from it. We can still solve the differential equations of motion numerically using a computer, of course. The solution is often chaotic though, so the positions of the bodies are hard to predict in the long term.

It turns out that, while the solutions to the two-body problem are just the conic sections, the problem with three bodies admits many different orbits with mesmerizing shapes. This is why I decided to write a short script to find examples of periodic orbits of three equal masses, using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm on a pseudospectral Fourier grid. In the video I show four of them, but of course you can try generating new ones with the code on GitHub!

Modular forms are complex functions that have a very particular symmetry under the modular group of transformations. They appear in many areas of mathematics, especially in number theory and combinatorics, and even in string theory and quantum gravity. They became especially famous as they were crucial for the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in 1994 by Andrew Wiles. They are so common that apparently Martin Eichler once said that "There are five fundamental operations in mathematics: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and modular forms."

Even though the definition of the forms is usually quite simple, plotting them can easily turn into a nightmare. This happens because they are usually defined in terms of infinite series whose value spans across many orders of magnitude in their domain. In order to plot them properly one must make use of their nice set of symmetries to compute them only on their "fundamental domain". The figure shows an example of a modular form, called the normalised Eisenstein series of weight 6. The color hue shows to the phase of the function, while each brightness contour corresponds to an order of magnitude in its modulus.
